How does sending to the sword work?

No cable, no Bluetooth: the editor blinks a little square, and the sword reads the blinks with a light sensor. Here's what happens, step by step.

1. The sword's eye: a light sensor

The sword has an LDR (light-dependent resistor) on pin PB4 of its ATtiny85 chip. Together with a resistor inside the chip it forms a voltage divider. The chip's ADC measures the voltage thousands of times a second as a number from 0 to 1023. More light means a lower number.

2. Light or dark? Self-calibrating

Every screen, room and sensor is different, so there's no fixed threshold. The sword keeps track of the lowest and highest readings of the last second, and puts the threshold halfway between them. Around it is a hysteresis band: to count as “lit” the reading has to drop below the band, and to count as “dark” again it has to rise above it. Small flickers inside the band change nothing.

Notice the curve: an LDR reacts to light within a few milliseconds, but takes much longer to recover in the dark. That's why the sword only times the moments the screen turns white, never when it turns black.

3. The handshake: “a sender is here”

While the sword shows its image, it keeps an eye on the sensor. Swinging past lamps makes random flashes, so it waits for something very regular: 10 slow flashes, half a second apart.

Once three intervals in a row match, the sword stops displaying, shows a light bar, and freezes its calibration. A steady half-second blink that goes on for too long isn't a sender either, so after 16 it gives up.

4. Training: how long is one unit?

The data is sent in units of a few screen frames each: the Transfer speed slider picks 2 to 6 frames per unit (33 to 100 ms at 60 frames a second). The sword doesn't know which, so the editor first sends a fixed pattern to measure it.

5. Symbols: it's all about timing

Every symbol is one unit of white followed by a gap. Only the length from one white flash to the next matters, and each length means something different. The sword decides with halfway points, so timing can be off by almost half a unit and still read correctly.

Faster works if the sensor and screen are quick enough; slower gives a slow sensor time to recover between flashes. That's the whole trade-off behind the slider.

7. Saving, and how the sword tells you

The image goes into the chip's EEPROM, memory that survives switching off. It's saved with a checksum, read back and checked, and only then marked valid by writing one last byte. A cut-off or garbled image is never shown. Long images are written column by column during the transfer: between two columns there's always enough time for the ~7 ms it takes to write one.

If it keeps failing: turn the screen brightness up, hold the sensor flat on the square, keep the tab in front, and move Transfer speed towards slower.
